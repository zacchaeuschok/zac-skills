# Logical Ordering

Every MECE group must be presented in a deliberate order. The order is not arbitrary. It reflects how the grouping was formed.

---

## Choose one ordering principle per group

| Ordering | When to use | Signal |
|----------|-------------|--------|
| **Time** | Items are steps in a process or events in a sequence | First → then → finally |
| **Structure** | Items are parts of a whole (geographic, organizational, conceptual) | North/South, Frontend/Backend, Input/Process/Output |
| **Importance** | Items are ranked by impact, urgency, or magnitude | Most critical → least critical |
| **Argument** | Items form a deductive chain (use sparingly, paragraph-level only) | Premise → premise → therefore |

## Time order

Use when the items are sequential actions or events. The reader should feel the flow of cause and effect.

- Step 1 enables Step 2.
- Step 2 enables Step 3.
- Reordering would break the logic.

**Test:** swap two items. If the sequence still makes sense, it is not true time order — consider importance order instead.

## Structural order

Use when the items are parts of a system, organization, or framework. The reader should see the whole divided into non-overlapping components.

Present in a consistent direction: top-down, left-right, outside-in, or general-to-specific. Never mix directions.

**Test:** could you draw a diagram where each item is a labeled box? If yes, structural order fits.

## Importance order

Use when the items are independent assertions ranked by impact. Lead with the strongest point. End with the weakest.

This is the default when neither time nor structure applies. Most key-line arguments use importance order.

**Test:** if the reader stopped reading after item 1, would they have the most critical information? If yes, importance order is correct.

## Argument order (deductive)

Use only at the paragraph level. Never at the key line.

A deductive chain requires the reader to hold all premises in memory before reaching the conclusion. At higher pyramid levels, this is too much cognitive load. At the paragraph level — three sentences — it works.

Pattern:
1. Major premise (general rule)
2. Minor premise (specific case that fits the rule)
3. Therefore (conclusion)

**Test:** does the conclusion follow necessarily from the premises? If the reader could accept both premises and still reject the conclusion, the deduction is invalid.
