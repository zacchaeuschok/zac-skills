# Sources of Power

A good strategy leverages real sources of power. These are the mechanisms through which strategy creates advantage. A strategy that contains a kernel but leverages no source of power is structurally correct but practically weak.

---

## Leverage

Focusing energy on pivotal objectives to produce cascading favorable outcomes. Three components:

**Anticipation** — Foreseeing predictable downstream results of trends and competitor behavior. Toyota invested $1B+ in hybrid technology during the SUV boom, anticipating fuel economy regulation. The insight was not the trend itself but its second-order consequences.

**Pivot points** — Natural or created imbalances where small adjustments yield outsized impact. Identify where a focused effort would produce disproportionate results. Not all objectives are equally pivotal.

**Concentration** — Focusing on fewer objectives generates larger payoffs through threshold effects. Completely turning around two schools may shift public opinion more than 2% improvement across two hundred. Resources spread thinly produce thin results.

**Test:** Does the strategy concentrate effort on one or two pivot points? Or does it spread resources across many fronts hoping something works?

## Proximate Objectives

Feasible targets close enough at hand to be achievable. A leader's most powerful tool.

- Absorb complexity, passing simpler problems to the organization
- In uncertain environments, objectives must be more proximate — long-range targets require long-range predictability
- Should be phrased as tasks, not goals
- Must unlock forward progress and create options for future moves

**Test:** Can the team accomplish this objective with known methods and available resources? If it requires invention or luck, it is not proximate.

**Example:** Kennedy's moon landing was proximate because the necessary technology existed. Success required coordination and resources, not breakthrough invention.

## Chain-Link Systems

Performance limited by the weakest subunit. Strengthening strong links while the weakest remains unchanged is wasted effort.

- Well-designed chain-link systems are extremely difficult to replicate
- Getting "unstuck" requires: shutting down normal measurement, refocusing on change itself, and sequencing improvements carefully
- Quality matching: when one link invests in improvement but others do not reciprocate, the system stays stuck

**Test:** What is the bottleneck? Does the strategy address it, or does it strengthen links that are already strong enough?

## Design

Treating strategy as design — mutual adjustment of elements into a coherent whole.

- Tighter integration yields higher performance but is harder to create and less flexible
- Most important when resources are constrained
- The power comes from the combination, not any single element

**Test:** Would removing one element weaken the others? If actions are independent, there is no design.

## Focus

Attacking a segment with a system that supplies more value than broad competitors can.

- Two components: coordinated policies producing power, and application of that power to the right target
- Identify policies differing from industry norms, determine what each accomplishes, seek reinforcement between them

**Test:** Is the strategy aimed at a specific target where the organization has disproportionate strength? Or does it attempt to serve everyone?

## Advantage

Rooted in asymmetries among rivals — differences in capabilities, resources, or positioning.

- Sources: lower production costs, higher perceived value, or a mix
- Sustained through isolating mechanisms: patents, reputation, network effects, scale, tacit knowledge, commercial relationships
- Advantages are context-specific — they apply to specific products, segments, and situations

**Test:** What specific asymmetry does this strategy exploit? What prevents competitors from replicating it?

## Dynamics

Industry-wide waves of change largely beyond any organization's control.

Five guideposts for detecting waves:
1. Rising fixed costs leading to consolidation
2. Deregulation creating opportunities
3. Predictable forecasting biases (assuming current leaders win, extrapolating growth)
4. Incumbent response patterns (resist transitions threatening accumulated positions)
5. Attractor states (where the industry "should" evolve based on efficiency)

**Key insight:** Focus on second-order effects. The microprocessor's rise was predicted. The resulting software demand explosion was not.

## Inertia and Entropy

**Inertia** — large competitors struggle to adapt. Three types:
- Routine inertia ("the way things are done here")
- Cultural inertia (stable social behaviors resistant to change)
- Proxy inertia (choosing not to adapt because current streams are profitable)

**Entropy** — organizations naturally become less organized and focused without active maintenance. Sloan's tightly coordinated GM product line decayed as brands expanded into competing price points.

**Test:** Does the strategy account for organizational inertia? Does it include active maintenance against entropy?
